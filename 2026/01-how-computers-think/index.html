An idea nobody needed for 80 years now runs every computer on Earth, including our robot.
  • Boole published The Laws of Thought in 1854. TRUE/FALSE = 1/0 = ON/OFF.
  • Shannon's 1937 thesis is often called the most important master's thesis ever written.
  • Michigan shout-out: born in Petoskey (1916), grew up in Gaylord, BS in electrical engineering and math from the University of Michigan (1936). Gaylord has a statue of him in a park. Ask who has been "up north" to Gaylord.

The switch inside every computer

A switch flipped by electricity: tubes, then transistors

1906

Vacuum tube

Grid: blocking → light OFF
Glass, hot, thumb-sized, burned out often. ENIAC needed about 18,000.
1947

Transistor

Base: no signal → light OFF
No glass, no heat-up, no moving parts. A modern chip has billions.
A tiny signal turns a big current on or off. Wire a few together and you get a logic gate.
  • Both panels flip on and off by themselves; click a switch to take control.
  • Vacuum tube (triode, Lee de Forest, 1906): a hot filament boils off electrons. The plate attracts them, so current flows and the light turns on. Put a negative voltage on the grid and it pushes the electrons back: current stops. Small signal on the grid, big current through the tube.
  • Problems: tubes are glass, big, hot and burn out. ENIAC had about 18,000; engineers were always replacing them (click the photo).
  • Transistor (Bell Labs, 1947: Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley; Nobel Prize 1956): same job, made of silicon. A small current into the Base lets a big current flow from Collector to Emitter. No heat-up, no moving parts, and they can be made microscopic.
  • Link: the processor on SystemCore (and the Raspberry Pi) has billions of transistors. Next slide: wire switches into gates.

Analog vs. digital · drag the sliders

The real world is smooth. Computers count in steps.

Analog (smooth)Digital (steps)

Analog

Smooth, any value in between. Like a ramp, a dimmer knob, or the hands on a clock.

Digital

Exact steps, stored as numbers. Like stairs, a light switch, or a digital clock. Copies perfectly, ignores noise.

◆ On the robotAnalog sensors (like a potentiometer on an arm) send a voltage. The controller's ADC (analog-to-digital converter) turns it into a number your code can use.
  • Analog = continuous: any value, smoothly changing (a ramp). Digital = discrete: exact steps (stairs). Computers can only store digital, so the real world gets measured and rounded.
  • Demo: drag "measurements" down to 4 and "bits" down to 1. The gold line becomes a crude square wave. Then drag both up: with 80 measurements and 8 bits (256 levels) it hugs the blue line. That's sampling and resolution, the same reason music files and phone photos look and sound smooth.
  • Why go digital at all? A copy of a number is perfect, and small noise doesn't change a 0 into a 1. A copy of an analog tape gets worse every time.
  • Robot tie-in: digital inputs read ON/OFF (limit switch, beam break). Analog inputs read a voltage (potentiometer, pressure sensor) and an ADC turns it into a number. On SystemCore, the I/O ports are run by a separate Raspberry Pi RP2350 chip that does this conversion.

What is a program?

Computers do exactly what you say, not what you mean

You say"Put peanut butter on the bread."
Computer doesPlaces the whole closed jar on top of the bag of bread. ✓ Done!

What it actually needed

1. Open the bread bag. 2. Take out one slice. 3. Unscrew the jar lid. 4. Pick up the knife. 5. Scoop peanut butter…

A program is a list of exact, step-by-step instructions. Computers are incredibly fast and incredibly literal. They never "know what you meant."

What happens when you press the power button?

    
From power on to your program, in five steps

    

      

        
Your programsbrowser, game, robot code

        
Shelldesktop or command line: how you talk to the OS

        
Kernelcore of the operating system

        
Boot loaderloads the OS

        
Firmware: BIOS / UEFItiny program in a chip on the board

        
HardwareCPU, RAM, storage, ports

      

      

        
1
Power on: firmware wakes upThe BIOS (or newer UEFI) is stored in a chip, so it runs even with nothing loaded. It checks the hardware (a "power-on self test") and finds the storage drive.

        
2
Boot loader starts the OSA small program on the drive copies the operating system's kernel from storage into RAM and starts it.

        
3
Kernel takes chargeThe kernel runs the whole time. It shares the CPU between programs, hands out memory, and talks to devices through drivers.

        
4
Shell: you can talk to itThe desktop (or a command-line terminal) appears. It's how you ask the OS to do things, like "open this program."

        
5
A program loads and runsThe OS copies the program from storage (pantry) into RAM (counter), and the CPU starts fetch, decode, execute. Each running program is called a process.

        
★ On our robotSystemCore boots the same way: firmware, boot loader, then the Linux kernel. Then it starts a program called the JVM, which loads and runs your robot code. More on that next session.

      

    

  

  
    
    
  • Each click lights the next layer on the left and explains it on the right.
    • 
    
  • Firmware: on older PCs it's called BIOS (Basic Input/Output System); newer PCs use UEFI. A Raspberry Pi (and SystemCore, which is built on one) keeps its boot firmware in a small EEPROM chip instead, but the job is the same: wake up, check, find the OS.
    • 
    
  • Boot loader: examples are GRUB on Linux PCs or Windows Boot Manager. Its only job is to get the kernel into RAM and hand over control.
    • 
    
  • Kernel: Windows, macOS and Linux each have one. It decides which program gets the CPU next (switching thousands of times a second, so everything seems to run at once), keeps programs from messing with each other's memory, and runs device drivers.
    • 
    
  • Shell: the "outer layer" around the kernel (kernel is the nut, shell is the shell). On a laptop that's the desktop; on SystemCore there's no screen, so mentors use a command-line shell over the network.
    • 
    
  • Process: a program that's been loaded into RAM and is running. Ask: how many are running on your laptop right now? (Open Task Manager or Activity Monitor: usually hundreds.)
    • 
    
  • Setup for next session: the JVM is just another program the OS loads. Then the JVM loads your bytecode. Two layers of "loading."
